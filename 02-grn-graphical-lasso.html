4  Undirected GGMs from single-cell transcriptomics

DeCovarT needs one sparse Gaussian graphical model per cell type at the population (pseudo-bulk) level, not a per-cell precision and not a directed regulatory DAG. The estimand, aligned with the article, is

\boldsymbol{x}_{\cdot j} \sim \mathcal{N}_{G}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\cdot j},\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j}\bigr), \qquad \boldsymbol{\Omega}_{j} \equiv \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j}^{-1} \ \text{sparse}, \tag{4.1}

with undirected edges encoding conditional dependence (Friedman et al. 2008; Mazumder and Hastie 2011). We write \boldsymbol{\Omega}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} for the precision and keep \theta for a generic parameter, not for a precision entry. Hats, checks and asterisks distinguish estimators from the truth:

  • \boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\ast} (entries \omega_{gg'}^{\ast}) is the true precision;
  • \widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}} is the sparse \ell_{1} estimator (graphical lasso or penalised PLN);
  • \check{\boldsymbol{\Omega}} is the de-sparsified matrix obtained by one Newton step from \widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}. Use the caron only when the two matrices could be confused: \check{\boldsymbol{\Omega}} is dense and intended for entrywise inference, not as the DeCovarT plug-in.

The object exported to the convolution is the sparse SPD matrix \widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{j} (or an SPD refit on an FDR support), on the linear scale of Equation 4.2.

Single-cell UMI profiles are the only observations. They are not the objects that enter the deconvolution likelihood: we use them to estimate (\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\cdot j},\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{j}) for labelled types in the Suppinger murine gastruloid resource (Suppinger et al. 2023). Downstream, those parameters discriminate types via mean shifts and precision rewiring, then enter the convolution

\boldsymbol{y}_{\cdot i} \mid (\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{p}_{\cdot i}) \sim \mathcal{N}_{G}\!\bigl( \boldsymbol{\mu}\,\boldsymbol{p}_{\cdot i},\, \textstyle\sum_{j}p_{ji}^{2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j} \bigr). \tag{4.2}

4.1 Scope of the core pipeline

In scope: undirected sparse precisions; single-cell transcriptomics only; stationarity; labelled cell types. The three estimators in Figure 1 are huge (graphical lasso), SILGGM after a nonparanormal map with global FDR, and PLNnetwork.

Dataset constraint. GSE229513 is a 10x Chromium 3′ v3 UMI resource with one technical scRNA-seq experiment, not independent biological replicates for GRNs (Suppinger et al. 2023). GSE250136 is a later stem-embryo dataset; it is external validation, not extra Suppinger replicates. Technical bootstrap of cells is allowed (Section 5.4); biological bootstrap is not. Networks are fit independently at 48 h, 72 h and 96 h (Section 2.1).

4.2 How each estimator sees the counts

A transformation belongs to one estimator. It is not crossed with the other two.

Estimator What is passed in Object exported
huge Continuous Gaussian table Sparse \widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{j}
SILGGM Nonparanormal scores, then global FDR Support, then an SPD refit
PLNnetwork Raw UMI counts and a library-size offset Latent Gaussian precision

SCTransform Pearson residuals stay in the HVG ranking of Section 3.2. Do not pass them to PLNnetwork: the PLN already has a Poisson sampling layer. The nonparanormal map is part of the SILGGM branch only (Note 4.1).

4.3 Three sparse-precision estimators

4.3.1 Graphical lasso in huge

huge is the maintained implementation of the graphical lasso of Friedman, Hastie and Tibshirani (Friedman et al. 2008; Mazumder and Hastie 2011; Zhao et al. 2012, 2022): a regularisation path, EBIC or StARS selection, and sparse-matrix output. The programme is the Friedman graphical lasso.

Generative model

Cells of one labelled type, at one time, are treated as independent Gaussian replicates. The precision is sparse.

Objective and assumptions

Let S be the sample covariance of the n cells on the gene panel. The estimator is

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}} = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}\succ 0} \Bigl[ -\log\det\boldsymbol{\Omega} + \operatorname{tr}(S\boldsymbol{\Omega}) + \lambda\|\boldsymbol{\Omega}\|_{1} \Bigr]. \tag{4.3}

Assumptions: multivariate Gaussian observations; conditional independence encoded by zeros of \boldsymbol{\Omega}; cells independent within the type-time subset; G may exceed n.

Optimisation and the \ell_{1} penalty

huge(method = "glasso") solves Equation 4.3 by graphical-lasso coordinate descent. The \ell_{1} term drives off-diagonal entries to zero. The diagonal is left unpenalised, so variances are not shrunk toward zero. A path of \lambda values is computed; one \lambda is then chosen.

Hyperparameters

  • \lambda path, and the selection rule in huge.select() (ebic (Foygel and Drton 2010) or stars (Liu et al. 2010));
  • whether the diagonal is penalised (default in this design: no);
  • the gene panel \mathcal{G}_{0} and the type-time subset.

4.3.2 SILGGM after a nonparanormal map

SILGGM tests entries of a Gaussian precision and controls FDR on the whole graph (Zhang et al. 2018a, 2018b). The arm used here is: nonparanormal scores, then de-sparsified graphical lasso (D-S_GL) with global = TRUE. With many cells the five SILGGM procedures tend to agree; at a bulk-sample size they need not. The object we keep is the SPD precision refit on the FDR support, not the dense de-sparsified matrix.

Note 4.1: ℹ️ Shrinkage versus truncation in huge.npn

Liu, Lafferty and Wasserman (Liu et al. 2009) define a nonparanormal vector by monotone maps f_{g} such that Z_{g}=f_{g}(X_{g}) and \boldsymbol{Z}\sim\mathcal{N}_{G}(\boldsymbol{0},R), with latent precision \boldsymbol{\Omega}=R^{-1}. By Sklar’s theorem, if the margins F_{g} are continuous,

F_{\boldsymbol{X}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{G}) = C_{R}\bigl(F_{1}(x_{1}),\ldots,F_{G}(x_{G})\bigr),

where C_{R} is the Gaussian copula with correlation R. Then U_{g}=F_{g}(X_{g})\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1) and

Z_{g} = \Phi^{-1}\bigl(F_{g}(X_{g})\bigr).

The unknown monotone margins change the observed scale, not the latent copula graph.

Let R_{ig}=\operatorname{rank}(X_{ig}) in a sample of size n. The raw empirical cdf \widehat{F}_{g}(X_{ig})=R_{ig}/n hits 1 at the sample maximum, and \Phi^{-1}(1)=+\infty. The score map is also unstable in the tails because

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}u}\Phi^{-1}(u) = \frac{1}{\phi\bigl(\Phi^{-1}(u)\bigr)}

diverges as u\downarrow 0 or u\uparrow 1. huge.npn() therefore regularises the plotting positions before \Phi^{-1} (Zhao et al. 2022). Both options address that instability; truncation is not intrinsically a “heavy-tail model”.

Shrinkage (npn.func = "shrinkage", the huge default, and ours) uses the plotting positions R_{ig}/(n+1):

\widetilde{Z}_{ig}^{\mathrm{shr}} = \Phi^{-1}\!\left(\frac{R_{ig}}{n+1}\right).

Attainable probabilities lie in \{1/(n+1),\ldots,n/(n+1)\}, so every Gaussian score stays finite and each distinct rank keeps a distinct score. Relative to R_{ig}/n the shift is at most 1/(n+1), not an O(n^{-1/2}) pull toward 1/2.

Truncation (npn.func = "truncation") winsorises R_{ig}/n at \delta_{n} before \Phi^{-1}. Unless npn.thresh is set, huge uses

\delta_{n} = \frac{1}{4\,n^{1/4}\sqrt{\pi\log n}} = O\bigl(n^{-1/4}(\log n)^{-1/2}\bigr),

not order n^{-1/2}. Central ranks are unchanged; all probabilities beyond the cutoff share the same endpoint score \Phi^{-1}(\delta_{n}) or \Phi^{-1}(1-\delta_{n}). That clips the tails more aggressively and discards rank information past the cutoff.

\begin{aligned} \text{shrinkage:} &\quad \widetilde{Z}_{ig} = \Phi^{-1}\!\left(\frac{R_{ig}}{n+1}\right), \\ \text{truncation:} &\quad \widetilde{Z}_{ig} = \Phi^{-1}\!\left[ \min\bigl\{1-\delta_{n},\, \max(\delta_{n},R_{ig}/n)\bigr\} \right]. \end{aligned} \tag{4.4}

huge.npn() then standardises columns. The output of shrinkage or truncation is an n\times G score matrix (skeptic returns a correlation matrix instead). Graphical lasso on those scores targets \boldsymbol{\Omega}=R^{-1} of the latent Gaussian \boldsymbol{Z}, not the precision of the raw counts. Means on this scale are not \boldsymbol{\mu}_{\cdot j}.

Generative model

Observed margins are monotone images of one latent Gaussian vector. The precision that SILGGM tests is the precision of that latent vector, not of the counts.

Objective and the de-sparsified correction

The first step is still Equation 4.3, which is \ell_{1}-biased. The Janková–van de Geer correction (Jankova and Geer 2015) is not 2\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}-S. On the correlation scale it is the one-step Newton update

\check{\boldsymbol{\Omega}} = 2\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}} - \widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\, \widehat{R}\, \widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}, \tag{4.5}

with \widehat{R} the empirical correlation of the NPN scores (replace \widehat{R} by \widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}} on the covariance scale). \check{\boldsymbol{\Omega}} recentres the edges so that they are asymptotically normal under a sparsity condition on the maximum degree. It is deliberately dense: it is an inferential object, not a generative precision. Zhang and Zou (Zhang and Zou 2014) note that graphical lasso remains useful for support recovery even when the Gaussian assumption fails. That supports edge screening, not export of \check{\boldsymbol{\Omega}} itself as \widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{j}.

Note 4.2: ℹ️ One Newton step removes the first-order \ell_{1} bias

The graphical-lasso KKT condition at \widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}} is

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}} - \widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{-1} + \lambda\widehat{\boldsymbol{Z}} = 0,

where \widehat{Z}_{gg'}\in[-1,1] is a subgradient of the \ell_{1} norm (and \widehat{Z}_{gg'}=\operatorname{sign}(\hat\omega_{gg'}) when \hat\omega_{gg'}\neq 0). The penalised estimator therefore does not solve the unpenalised score g(\boldsymbol{\Omega})=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}-\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{-1}=0. The leftover score \lambda\widehat{\boldsymbol{Z}} is the first-order shrinkage bias.

The Fréchet derivative of g at \boldsymbol{\Omega} maps H\mapsto\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{-1}H\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{-1}, so its inverse sends A\mapsto\boldsymbol{\Omega}A\boldsymbol{\Omega}. One Newton step from \widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}} is therefore

\begin{aligned} \check{\boldsymbol{\Omega}} &= \widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}} - \widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}} \bigl(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{-1}\bigr) \widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}} \\ &= 2\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}} - \widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\, \widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\, \widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}, \end{aligned}

which is Equation 4.5 on the covariance scale. Equivalently, \check{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}+\lambda\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{Z}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}: the correction adds back the KKT bias term.

Under the usual sparsity and rate conditions the remainder after this linear expansion is o_{p}(n^{-1/2}), so a single Newton step is enough for entrywise \sqrt{n}-normality (Jankova and Geer 2015):

\sqrt{n}\, (\check\omega_{gg'}-\omega_{gg'}^{\ast}) \;\xrightarrow{d}\; \mathcal{N}\bigl(0,\; \omega_{gg}^{\ast}\omega_{g'g'}^{\ast} +(\omega_{gg'}^{\ast})^{2} \bigr).

The plug-in standard error is \widehat{\mathrm{se}}(\check\omega_{gg'}) =\sqrt{(\hat\omega_{gg}\hat\omega_{g'g'}+\hat\omega_{gg'}^{2})/n}. SILGGM (D-S_GL, global = TRUE) thresholds these de-sparsified z-statistics by the Liu–et-al. global FDR rule at \alpha=0.05 (Liu 2013). The resulting support is sparse; \check{\boldsymbol{\Omega}} itself stays dense. DeCovarT then refits an SPD \widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{j} on that support.

Optimisation and global FDR

SILGGM runs a lasso-type fit, builds the debiased statistics, and, with global = TRUE, thresholds them by the global FDR rule for Gaussian graphical models (Liu 2013; Zhang et al. 2018a). The selected zeros define a support. An SPD precision is then refit on that support (graphical lasso constrained to the support, or a small ridge if the constrained MLE is unavailable). Edge testing and the DeCovarT precision stay separate (Equation 4.1).

Hyperparameters

  • npn.func: shrinkage (default) or truncation (Note 4.1);
  • method = "D-S_GL" and global = TRUE;
  • FDR level \alpha=0.05;
  • lambda if it is not left at the package default.

4.3.3 Poisson-lognormal network

PLNnetwork is the sparse-precision member of PLNmodels (Chiquet et al. 2021, 2018, 2025). Fused PLN graphical lasso is a perspective (?sec-grn-appendix), not this arm.

Generative model

For each cell c in one type-time subset, with library size S_{c},

Y_{cg}\mid\boldsymbol{Z}_{c} \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(S_{c}Z_{cg}), \qquad \log\boldsymbol{Z}_{c} \sim \mathcal{N}_{G}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}), \quad \boldsymbol{\Omega}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}. \tag{4.6}

Zeros are Poisson sampling zeros (Svensson 2020). The fit is independent for each labelled type at each of 48 h, 72 h and 96 h. A single formula with cell-type and time dummies would estimate one shared precision and only shift the mean; that is not the DeCovarT target. The formula interface is used inside each subset, with an offset and no further design.

Objective and assumptions

The observed-data likelihood integrates the latent Gaussian. That integral is intractable. Variational inference replaces the posterior of \boldsymbol{Z} by a tractable q and maximises the evidence lower bound, which is the expected complete log-likelihood minus \mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p(Z\mid Y)). An \ell_{1} penalty is added on off-diagonal entries of \boldsymbol{\Omega}.

Important 4.3: ℹ️ Variational EM, KL, and why not Monte Carlo

EM alternates an expectation under the true conditional p(Z\mid Y) with a maximisation of the expected complete log-likelihood. When that conditional is unavailable, variational EM restricts the expectation to a family q. The identity

\log p(Y) = \mathrm{ELBO}(q) + \mathrm{KL}\bigl(q\,\|\,p(Z\mid Y)\bigr)

shows that maximising the ELBO is the same as minimising the Kullback–Leibler divergence from q to the true posterior. Equality holds when q=p(Z\mid Y), which is ordinary EM. A Monte Carlo E-step would draw the latent Gaussian at every iteration. The variational step replaces those draws by a closed-form update of q, which is why the same sparse PLN is practical at single-cell n (Chiquet et al. 2018).

Optimisation and the \ell_{1} penalty

PLNnetwork() runs that variational scheme over a grid of penalties (PLNnetwork). The pair-specific graphical-lasso weight is \rho_{gg'}=\lambda w_{gg'}. penalize_diagonal stays FALSE in control_main, matching the graphical-lasso convention. The penalty grid is set by penalties, or by nPenalties and min.ratio in control_init.

The inner continuous problem in (B,M,S) at fixed \boldsymbol{\Omega} is solved by NLopt. We keep the conservative quadratic solver CCSAQ as the default algorithm (Chiquet et al. 2018).

\begin{algorithm} \caption{PLNnetwork alternating variational / graphical-lasso optimisation} \begin{algorithmic} \Require Counts $Y$, covariates $X$, offsets $O$, penalty $\lambda$ \Require penalty weights $W$ \Require inner tolerances $(f_{\mathrm{rel}},f_{\mathrm{abs}}, x_{\mathrm{rel}},x_{\mathrm{abs}})$ and \texttt{maxeval} \Require outer tolerance \texttt{ftol\_out} and \texttt{maxit\_out} \State Initialize $(B,M,S,\Sigma)$ \State $\rho \gets \lambda W$ \For{$h = 1,\ldots,\texttt{maxit\_out}$} \State $\Omega^{(h)} \gets \Call{GraphicalLasso}{\Sigma^{(h-1)},\rho}$ \Comment{precision / sparsity update} \State $(B^{(h)},M^{(h)},S^{(h)}) \gets \Call{NLOPT}{ B^{(h-1)},M^{(h-1)},S^{(h-1)}; \Omega^{(h)} }$ \Comment{inner CCSAQ solve} \Statex \hspace{\algorithmicindent} \Comment{NLOPT stops by ftol\_rel/abs, xtol\_rel/abs or maxeval} \State $\Sigma^{(h)} \gets n^{-1}\left[ {M^{(h)}}^\top M^{(h)} + \sum_i S_i^{(h)} \right]$ \State $F_h \gets -\mathrm{ELBO}(B^{(h)},M^{(h)},S^{(h)},\Omega^{(h)}) + \lambda\|\Omega^{(h)}\|_{1,W}$ \If{$|F_h-F_{h-1}|/|F_h| < \texttt{ftol\_out}$} \State \textbf{break} \EndIf \EndFor \State \Return $(B,M,S,\Omega,\Sigma)$ \end{algorithmic} \end{algorithm}

Algorithm 4.1 is the block-coordinate scheme of Chiquet et al. (2018): graphical lasso updates \boldsymbol{\Omega}, then NLopt updates (B,M,S). Inner stopping uses ftol_*, xtol_* and maxeval; outer stopping uses ftol_out and maxit_out.

Hyperparameters

Inside each type-time subset the call is of the form abundance ~ 1 + offset(log_library), with log_library the natural log of that cell’s library size. Table 4.1 lists the knobs that change either sparsity or numerical convergence.

CCSAQ is a conservative quadratic separable approximation with bound constraints; NLopt recommends trying it before MMA. LBFGS, VAR1 and VAR2 remain alternatives if the inner solve is the bottleneck, not the default.

Zero-inflated PLN is not the default (Note 4.5).

Note 4.4: ℹ️ The offset compares library-size-adjusted rates

In the simplest Poisson GLM for a gene g and cell (or sample) c,

Y_{gc}\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\mu_{gc}), \qquad \log\mu_{gc} = \log L_{c} + \beta_{0g} + \beta_{1g}X_{c},

the term \log L_{c} is an offset: its coefficient is fixed at one. The mean therefore factors as \mu_{gc}=L_{c}\,q_{gc} with rate q_{gc}=\exp(\beta_{0g}+\beta_{1g}X_{c}). The response remains the raw count; the offset makes the condition coefficient a log ratio of rates,

\beta_{1g} = \log(q_{gB}/q_{gA}),

not a log ratio of raw read counts. PLNnetwork uses the same device in Equation 4.6: S_{c} (library size) multiplies the latent intensity, so the latent Gaussian \log\boldsymbol{Z}_{c} is a model for relative abundance after sequencing exposure.

Figure 4.8 shows the two-condition cartoon. Equal rates and a doubled library yield doubled counts but \beta_{1}=0. A 1.5-fold rate increase at doubled depth yields \beta_{1}=\ln(1.5)\approx0.405 (\log_{2} fold change \approx 0.585).

McCarthy, Chen and Smyth write the RNA-seq GLM with the (effective) library size as an offset in the linear predictor, so that the condition coefficient is a log ratio of relative abundances after sequencing depth (McCarthy et al. 2012). DESeq2 uses the same multiplicative mean \mu_{gc}=s_{c}q_{gc}, with s_{c} a size factor and q_{gc} the abundance that carries the fold change (Love et al. 2014). In PLN we pass offset(log_library) rather than transforming Y. The offset is relative normalisation, not conversion to absolute molecule counts.

4.4 Choosing the penalty

Four displays, and only these, are used to pick the penalty for each of the three estimators. Partial-correlation weights matter, so heatmaps show the precision, not a binary adjacency.

4.4.1 Stability criteria

These plots decide \lambda (huge), \alpha (SILGGM; we use 0.05), or the PLN penalty grid. They are not the final cell-type report.

  1. Regularisation paths. Plot each off-diagonal entry of \widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}(\lambda) against \lambda or \log\lambda. This is the classical lasso path: which edges enter as the penalty relaxes.
  2. EBIC against \lambda. Plot \mathrm{EBIC}_{\gamma}(\lambda) against \log\lambda, with a line at the minimiser (Foygel and Drton 2010). Overlay \gamma\in\{0,0.25,0.5\} when that hyperparameter is free. A flat valley means many penalties are equivalent.
  3. StARS-style instability (Section 6.1). For huge, huge.select(..., criterion = "stars") is the subsample edge-instability rule; we reuse the same idea as a diagnostic for the other two estimators.
  4. Edge-count against EBIC. Put the number of edges on the horizontal axis and EBIC on the vertical axis, and mark the optimum. That is the sparsity–fit tradeoff without depending on the numerical scale of \lambda.

For SILGGM, repeat the same pictures with the FDR level in place of \lambda when a path of \alpha is computed. For PLNnetwork, the horizontal axis is the penalty grid in Table 4.1.

4.4.2 Descriptive plots per cell type

Once a penalty is fixed, two figure folders (not a single mixed gallery) report the selected networks.

Folder 1 — one type, one time, all estimators. A funkyheatmap table with rows = cell types (or type \times time) and columns = global scores of each selected network: EBIC, Hellinger distance to the empirical cell cloud, mean off-diagonal edge density, number of edges, and a degree-distribution summary (mean degree; a bar plot of degrees sits beside the heatmap, never as a transcriptome hairball). Partial-correlation heatmaps at the selected penalty use a frozen gene order (cluster once) and

\rho_{gg'\mid\mathrm{rest}} = -\frac{\omega_{gg'}}{\sqrt{\omega_{gg}\omega_{g'g'}}}. \tag{4.7}

Blocks that survive a stronger penalty are the stable ones.

Folder 2 — pairwise type comparisons. The same global scores, now as pairwise contrasts (difference or ratio of EBIC, Hellinger, density, ) between cell types at a fixed time, again with funkyheatmap. This is the rewiring view at the metric level; signed partial-correlation difference heatmaps belong with the visualisations in Section 6.3.

4.5 Three core estimators

This chapter’s product is the three precision estimators, not the full 3\times 2\times 2 design of Figure 1 (lifts and gene panels are later chapters).

\{\texttt{huge},\; \texttt{SILGGM+NPN},\; \texttt{PLNnetwork}\}. \tag{4.8}

Every core method must finally export

\bigl\{ \widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\cdot j},\, \widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{j},\, \widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{j} \bigr\}_{j=1}^{J}, \qquad \widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{j} = \widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{j}^{-1} \succ 0, \tag{4.9}

on the linear scale of Equation 4.2, not on NPN scores.

The knobs that Section 4.4 is meant to set are \lambda (huge), \alpha=0.05 (SILGGM), and the PLN penalty grid.

Warning 4.5: ⚠️ Why we drop zero-inflated directed graphs in the core

Directed zero-inflated DAGs are the wrong object for DeCovarT: the output is a DAG, not an undirected SPD precision. Suppinger is droplet UMI data. Svensson showed that zeros in droplet scRNA-seq are often explained by ordinary molecule-sampling counts (Svensson 2020). Keep hurdle or ZINB graphs in Section 10.1 if the observation model is to be contaminated, not as a GRN estimator.

4.5.1 Cartesian GmGM cannot separate within-cell edges

GmGM replaces independent cells by a Cartesian product: gene g in cell c may depend on other genes in the same cell, or on the same gene in other cells, but not on an arbitrary pair (c,g)\neq(c',g') (Andrew et al. 2026). The precision of the vectorised matrix is a Kronecker sum of a gene precision and a cell precision. That sum forbids the cross terms that would distinguish regulation inside a cell from dependence across cells.

A strong-product Gaussian graphical model is the extension that can carry both within-cell and across-cell edges. It is not available as a drop-in for this design. Figure 4.9 sketches why labelled types still do not rescue the Cartesian support: hard cell-type blocks remove cross-type edges, but inside a type the Cartesian sum still cannot separate intracellular regulation from cell–cell dependence.

4.5.2 Sparse plus low-rank precisions

Unmeasured programmes can make the marginal precision dense. Chandrasekaran, Parrilo and Willsky split it into a sparse conditional graph minus a low-rank factor term (Chandrasekaran et al. 2012),

\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{marg}} = \boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{sparse}} - \boldsymbol{L}, \qquad \boldsymbol{L}\succeq 0. \tag{4.10}

A useful reading of \boldsymbol{L} is a small set of transcription factors, or of co-regulated pathways, that lower the rank of the residual dependence. \boldsymbol{L} is a nuisance for DeCovarT, not a type covariance. This check is not in the 3\times 2\times 2 product.

4.5.3 Fused graphical lasso on a PLN

Related types can share part of a graph. The joint graphical lasso (Danaher et al. 2012) penalises both sparsity and the gap between \omega_{gg'}^{(j)} and \omega_{gg'}^{(\ell)}. PLNFGL puts a Poisson-lognormal sampling layer under that fused penalty (Zhai et al. 2026). Fusion can shrink away the rewiring DeCovarT is meant to keep, so it stays out of the independent-type comparison.

4.5.4 Other copula joints

JGCGM learns shared and subgroup-specific edges under a Gaussian copula and warns against a naive nonparanormal map on counts (Wu et al. 2020). It does not replace PLNnetwork on UMI counts. Structured penalties from earlier Chiquet work (Chiquet et al. 2012) could encode pathway modules; they are optional regularisers, not a fourth core estimator.
